# Basketball Training Session Framework

---

## 1. Five-Phase Session Architecture — Overview

Section summary: A well-structured basketball training session has five phases — activation, skill development, competitive application, conditioning, and cool-down — each with a distinct purpose and time allocation; deviating from this structure reduces both learning quality and physical safety.

Every training session, regardless of focus, season phase, or level of competition, is most effective when it follows the five-phase structure below. Adjust phase durations based on the session's primary focus (skill-heavy vs. conditioning-heavy) and the team's competition schedule. Never sacrifice the warm-up or cool-down phases — injury prevention is more cost-effective than rehabilitation.

| Phase | Name | Duration | Primary purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Activation and Warm-Up | 10–15 min | Elevate heart rate, prepare joints, focus attention |
| 2 | Skill Development | 20–30 min | Isolated high-repetition technical work |
| 3 | Competitive Application | 20–30 min | Transfer skills to game-like pressure contexts |
| 4 | Physical Conditioning | 10–20 min | Build fitness when players are already fatigued from skill work |
| 5 | Cool-Down and Recovery | 5–10 min | Accelerate recovery, debrief, set next-session context |

**Total session length:** 65–115 minutes depending on phase emphasis.

**Skill-heavy session (e.g., early pre-season):** Extend Phases 2 and 3; reduce Phase 4 to 10 minutes.
**Conditioning-heavy session (e.g., late pre-season fitness block):** Extend Phase 4 to 20 minutes; reduce Phase 2 to 20 minutes.
**Game-day preparation session (day before a game):** Phases 1, 2, and 5 only (maximum 60 minutes total). No Phase 4.

---

## 2. Phase 1 — Activation and Warm-Up

Section summary: The warm-up phase elevates heart rate, prepares the joints most stressed in basketball (ankles, knees, hips), focuses player attention on the session theme, and reduces acute soft-tissue injury risk.

**Duration:** 10–15 minutes.

**Execution sequence:**

- **General movement (5 minutes):** Light jogging, side shuffles, and back-pedals around the court perimeter. The goal is a slight sweat before stretching — cold muscles should not be statically stretched.
- **Dynamic stretching sequence (5 minutes):**
  - Leg swings: forward and lateral, 10 repetitions each leg.
  - Hip circles: 10 repetitions each direction.
  - Ankle rotations: 10 repetitions each ankle.
  - Thoracic rotations: 5 repetitions each side (hands behind the head, rotate upper back).
  - Arm circles: 10 repetitions each direction, small and large range of motion.
- **Basketball-specific activation (3–4 minutes):**
  - Zig-zag defensive slides: full-court, changing direction at each lane line.
  - Skip passes in motion: two lines of players passing a ball to each other while jogging toward the basket and back.
  - Two-ball dribbling at low intensity for three minutes (both hands simultaneously, then alternating).
- **Cognitive activation cue (final 60 seconds):** The coach states the session's focus theme in one sentence (e.g., "Today's focus is on our closeout footwork — every rep, every closeout arrives with short steps and high hands"). This primes players' attention for the upcoming skill work and creates accountability for the session's learning objective.

**Coaching note:** The cognitive activation cue is not optional. Research on attention in skill acquisition shows that naming the specific focus of a session increases on-task behaviour during drills. Make it a habit regardless of level or age group.

---

## 3. Phase 2 — Skill Development

Section summary: The skill development block isolates one or two specific technical skills with high repetition volume; it progresses from stationary to moving to contextualised execution within the same block.

**Duration:** 20–30 minutes.

**Core principles:**

- **Repetition volume:** Target 50–100 quality repetitions of the focal skill per player per session. Quality means correct technique — stop and correct errors before they become ingrained habits.
- **Drill progression within Phase 2:** Begin stationary → add movement → add passive defence (a shadow defender) → add active, live competition. Not all four stages need to be reached in one session for beginning players; advance only when the current stage is executed with 70 percent or higher correctness.
- **Feedback interval:** Provide individual feedback every three to five repetitions. Do not wait until the end of the drill block to correct fundamental errors. Delayed feedback during early-stage skill acquisition reduces learning rate.
- **Blocked vs. random practice:**
  - Use blocked practice (the same movement repeated identically) for early-stage skill learning (new players, new skills).
  - Use random practice (alternating between two or three different skills in random order) for players who have already acquired the skill but are refining it for game-like conditions. Random practice is harder and less satisfying but produces more durable retention.
- **End on a success:** In Phase 2, always conclude the drill block with a repetition or sequence that the player can complete correctly at the current level. Ending on a failure leaves a negative motor memory as the last encode of the session.

---

## 4. Phase 3 — Competitive Application

Section summary: The competitive application phase transfers the skill trained in Phase 2 to game-like environments under competitive pressure; small-sided games and constraint-based drills are the primary tools.

**Duration:** 20–30 minutes.

**Small-sided game formats:**
- Use 2-on-2, 3-on-3, or 4-on-4 formats rather than full 5-on-5. Small-sided games increase individual ball touches per minute, increase individual decision-making repetitions, and allow coaches to observe and correct individual technique in a live context.
- Full 5-on-5 is appropriate for system-level work (running full plays, practising transition sequences) but is less efficient for individual skill transfer.

**Constraint-based drills:**
- Impose rule constraints that force the skill trained in Phase 2 to emerge naturally from the game situation.
- Examples:
  - "Every possession must include a ball screen" (forces P&R reads).
  - "Every shot must be a catch-and-shoot — no off-the-dribble shots allowed" (forces movement off the ball and pass timing).
  - "No dribble allowed after the catch on the wing" (forces pass-fake, shot fake, or immediate drive decision).
- Constraints create game-like decisions while keeping the volume of the target skill high.

**Score and compete:**
- Attach stakes to every competitive drill. Even a small consequence (losing team runs a sprint, losing team does push-ups, winning team earns an extra minute off conditioning) raises the cognitive load to approximate game conditions. Low-stakes drills do not prepare players for the emotional context of competition.

**Post-segment debrief:**
- Pause for 60–90 seconds after each competitive segment to review one specific execution point.
- Do not deliver lengthy speeches or multiple correction points during the competitive phase. One clear, specific coaching point per pause.
- Example debrief format: "What I saw — [specific observation]. What we need — [specific correction]. Next rep, let's see [specific desired action]."

---

## 5. Phase 4 — Physical Conditioning

Section summary: Conditioning is placed after skill work so that players learn skills when physically fresh and train fitness under fatigue, which more accurately replicates late-game physical demands.

**Duration:** 10–20 minutes.

**Key principle:** Basketball conditioning should replicate the game's physical demands: short, intense bursts of two to five seconds followed by partial recovery periods of 15 to 30 seconds, repeated across 40 minutes of game time. RPE = Rate of Perceived Exertion on a 1–10 scale. Target RPE 7–8 for standard conditioning work.

**Anaerobic capacity (sprint intervals):**
- 4 × court sprints from baseline to baseline with 20-second rest between sprints.
- Repeat four sets total (16 total sprints). Target RPE 8–9.
- Coaching cue: players must accelerate maximally from the first step — no slow starts.

**Lateral quickness (defensive slides):**
- Defensive slide ladder: player slides laterally between the near lane line and the near sideline.
- Work interval: 30 seconds. Rest interval: 15 seconds. Four rounds.
- Add a ball-shadow element (a partner holds a ball and moves laterally; the defender mirrors) to increase game specificity.

**Change of direction (5-10-5 pro-agility):**
- Player starts at the centre cone. Sprints five metres to one cone, pivots, sprints 10 metres to the far cone, pivots, sprints five metres back to the centre.
- Eight repetitions per player. Rest 30 seconds between repetitions.
- Focus coaching cue: low hips at each pivot, shin angle forward in the first step after the pivot.

**Aerobic maintenance (recovery sessions):**
- On recovery days or the day after a game, replace all sprint work with a 15-minute continuous movement drill at RPE 5–6.
- Example: full-court shell defence at moderate pace — five defenders rotate through positions while four offensive players pass and move. No competitive scoring, no sprints.

---

## 6. Phase 5 — Cool-Down and Recovery

Section summary: The cool-down phase accelerates physical recovery, reduces next-day soreness through static stretching and breathing regulation, and closes the session with a structured team debrief that reinforces learning.

**Duration:** 5–10 minutes.

**Static stretching sequence (hold each stretch 30 seconds per side):**
- Quadriceps stretch: standing, pull heel to glute.
- Hamstring stretch: seated, reach toward both feet.
- Hip flexor lunge stretch: rear knee on ground, front foot forward, shift hips forward.
- Calf stretch: rear-foot straight, heel flat on ground, lean forward.
- Shoulder cross-body stretch: pull one arm across the chest with the opposite hand.

**Breathing regulation (1–2 minutes):**
- Slow diaphragmatic breathing: four seconds inhale (belly out), hold two seconds, six seconds exhale (belly in).
- Purpose: activates the parasympathetic nervous system, lowers heart rate, and reduces cortisol. This is physiologically effective, not optional.

**Team debrief (2–3 minutes in a huddle):**
- Format: the coach reviews one specific positive observation and one specific area for improvement.
- Rules: keep it brief (under 90 seconds total), keep it specific (name the action and the player if appropriate), and keep it forward-looking ("Next time we see this, we…").
- Do not use the debrief to replay all errors from the session. The last thing players hear before leaving practice has disproportionate weight in their retention — make it constructive and clear.

## 7. Shooting Mechanics Checkpoints

Section summary: This section lists the five mechanical checkpoints for correct shooting form; these checkpoints are used to audit and correct a player's shooting technique before progressing to game-speed shooting drills.

Do not advance a player to game-speed shooting until they can execute the stationary form correctly on nine out of 10 attempts at close range (1.5 metres). Use the checkpoints below as the audit framework.

**Checkpoint 1 — Stance:**
- Feet shoulder-width apart.
- Shooting foot (same side as shooting hand) is slightly forward.
- Knees are slightly bent before the shot is initiated — the bend pre-loads the leg drive.

**Checkpoint 2 — Grip:**
- Ball rests on the finger pads, not the palm. Visible space between palm and ball.
- The non-shooting hand (guide hand) is on the side of the ball — not underneath.
- The guide hand is removed before the ball is released. A guide hand that pushes the ball forward causes side-spin and drift.

**Checkpoint 3 — Alignment:**
- Shooting elbow is directly under the ball and aligned with the basket. A flared elbow causes the ball to drift off the shooting-hand side.
- The wrist is cocked back at 90 degrees before the shooting motion begins. A flat wrist reduces the snap at release and shortens the shot arc.

**Checkpoint 4 — Upward force and release:**
- Legs drive upward first. Energy transfers sequentially from legs → core → shooting arm. Do not initiate the arm before the leg drive.
- The ball is released at the peak of the jump (or at full arm extension for a set shot).
- The wrist snaps fully forward at release — fingers point toward the basket at follow-through.

**Checkpoint 5 — Follow-through:**
- Hold the follow-through position (the "gooseneck" wrist shape — fingers pointing downward toward the basket) until the ball hits the rim or net.
- Eyes stay on the target (the back of the rim or the back of the backboard square) through the full motion.
- A dropped follow-through before the ball lands is the single most common mechanical flaw at youth levels; address it before all others.

**Shooting drill progression (post-checkpoint):**
- Level 1: Form shooting at 1.5 metres — close-range repetition for mechanical reinforcement.
- Level 2: Mid-range catch-and-shoot from five spots (both corners, both wings, top of key).
- Level 3: Three-point catch-and-shoot from five spots.
- Level 4: Off-the-dribble pull-up from mid-range — one dribble left, one dribble right.
- Level 5: Game-speed shooting off screens and hand-offs in a competitive context.

Each level requires 70 percent or higher make rate before advancing to the next level.

## 8. Footwork and Finishing Techniques

Section summary: This section details the five key footwork and finishing techniques that reduce offensive fouls and improve finishing percentage at the rim; each technique is described with the footwork sequence and a key coaching cue.

**Two-step layup footwork:**
- Right-hand layup: left foot contact, right foot contact, jump off the left foot. The final jump foot is the opposite of the shooting hand.
- Left-hand layup: right foot contact, left foot contact, jump off the right foot.
- Drill at half speed before adding game pace. Common error: jumping off the same foot as the shooting hand, which causes body lean and missed layups.

**Euro-step:**
- First step attacks one lateral direction (e.g., right).
- Second step crosses to the opposite direction (left), taking the defender's momentum with it.
- The ball is cradled between steps — not passed, not dribbled. Any ball movement between steps is a travel.
- Used to navigate help defenders in the paint after beating the on-ball defender on the drive.
- Coaching cue: "Attack, gather, cross" — three words to teach the rhythm.

**Reverse layup:**
- The player passes under the rim and finishes on the far side of the basket, using the rim as a physical shield against shot-blockers.
- Jump off the near foot (the foot closest to the basket on the approach), extend the far hand for the finish.
- Used against rim-protecting centres who have positioned themselves between the driver and the basket.

**Jump stop:**
- A two-foot simultaneous landing that establishes a pivot foot by choice (either foot can be designated).
- Gives the ball-handler more time to read the defence after catching the ball than a one-foot stop because neither foot is committed as the pivot until the player makes a deliberate pivot.
- Used to avoid charging fouls by stopping short of a set defender.
- Coaching cue: "Land together" — both feet must hit the floor at the same moment or it is a travel.

**Pivot drills:**
- Front pivot: rotate the body forward (away from the defender) on the pivot foot.
- Reverse pivot: rotate the body backward (into the defender's blind side) on the pivot foot.
- Practise with and without a ball, starting stationary before adding a catch in traffic.
- A clean pivot prevents charging fouls and protects the ball from the on-ball defender.

## 9. Strength and Power Development for Basketball

Section summary: This section outlines the basketball-specific resistance training framework by movement category and load range; physical training complements on-court sessions and must be sequenced to avoid conflicting with game preparation.

Basketball demands explosive power, lateral speed, aerobic endurance, and injury-resistant joint stability across a full competitive season. Physical training must address all four qualities in the appropriate proportion for the current season phase. 1RM = One-Repetition Maximum (the most weight a player can lift for a single repetition of an exercise). ROM = Range of Motion.

**Lower-body strength (priority 1):**
- Movements: back squat, front squat, trap-bar deadlift.
- Load and volume: four to six repetitions at 80–85 percent of 1RM.
- Primary adaptation: maximal force production — the base of all jump height and lateral power.

**Lower-body power (priority 2):**
- Movements: hang power clean, squat jump, box jump.
- Load and volume: three to five repetitions at maximum intent with 40–60 percent of 1RM (light loads moved explosively).
- Primary adaptation: rate of force development — converting strength into fast-twitch speed.

**Single-leg stability (priority 3):**
- Movements: Bulgarian split squat, single-leg Romanian deadlift, lateral step-up.
- Load and volume: eight to 12 repetitions at moderate load.
- Primary adaptation: ankle and knee injury prevention — the most cost-effective physical investment for basketball players.

**Upper-body pushing:**
- Movements: bench press, push-up variations, landmine press.
- Coaching note: basketball requires shoulder stability more than raw pressing strength. Include rotator-cuff activation work (external rotation with band, Y-T-W raises) in every upper-body session.

**Upper-body pulling:**
- Movements: pull-up, seated cable row, face pull.
- Coaching note: pulling strength balances the pressing work and reduces shoulder-impingement risk. Maintain at least a 1:1 ratio of pulling to pushing volume.

**Core stability:**
- Movements: front plank, lateral plank, Pallof press, dead bug.
- Coaching note: basketball core training is about stiffness and force transfer, not flexion-based sit-up movements. A rigid, stable core transfers leg power to the shooting arm and protects the spine during contact.

## 10. Speed and Agility Development

Section summary: This section details basketball-specific speed and agility methods that address the lateral, reactive, and directional demands of the game — demands that differ fundamentally from straight-line sprint training.

Basketball speed is directional: lateral sliding, back-pedalling, and diagonal first-step explosions matter more than peak straight-line sprint speed. Train accordingly.

**Linear speed:**
- Drill: Flying 10-metre sprint — athlete accelerates for 10 metres from a rolling start. Measures acceleration mechanics, not top speed.
- Coaching cues: arm drive and shin angle in the first five metres. Short, fast ground contacts.

**Lateral speed:**
- Drill: Defensive slide series — two metres left, two metres right, four metres left, four metres right (progressively wider). Continue for 30 seconds per set.
- Game-specific addition: add a ball-shadow element — the player mirrors a partner who holds a ball and moves laterally without verbal cues. The defender reacts to body movement, not verbal signals.

**Reactive agility:**
- Drill: Mirror drill — two players face each other. The lead player moves in any direction without a predetermined pattern. The follower mirrors the movement as quickly as possible.
- Why it matters: mirror drills are more game-specific than pre-planned cone drills because they train reactive rather than anticipatory movement. In-game defenders never know which direction the dribbler will go.

**First-step explosion:**
- Drill: Partner-resisted lateral start — a partner places gentle pressure on the defender's shoulder while the defender is in a defensive stance. On the partner's release, the defender accelerates laterally at maximum speed.
- Coaching note: the resistance and release develop fast-twitch muscle activation — the quick neural firing pattern that produces the first defensive step.

## 11. Injury Prevention Protocols

Section summary: This section details the three primary injury-prevention protocols for basketball — ankle stability, knee health, and hip flexor care — including the specific exercises and frequencies that are evidence-supported for each.

Ankle, knee, and hip-flexor injuries are the most common in basketball. A structured prevention programme reduces injury rates significantly when performed consistently. These protocols should be embedded in the warm-up or cool-down phase, not treated as a separate workout.

**Ankle stability protocol (daily):**
- Single-leg balance: 30 seconds each foot, eyes open. Progress to eyes closed, then to a balance board.
- Resistance-band eversion and inversion: three sets of 15 repetitions each direction.
- Proprioceptive board work: one to two minutes each foot — maintain balance while making small corrections.

**Knee health protocol (twice per week):**
- Nordic hamstring curls: three sets of five to eight repetitions, slow eccentric phase (take four seconds to lower the body).
- This movement is the most evidence-based single intervention for hamstring and knee injury prevention in court sports.
- Progress from assisted (using a partner for partial support) to unassisted.

**Hip flexor care (after any sprint-heavy session):**
- Hip-flexor couch stretch: 90 seconds each side (rear knee on the floor, front foot elevated, hips pressed forward).
- Psoas activation release: 60 seconds each side.
- Do not skip this protocol after any session containing repeated sprint volume — hip flexor tightness accumulates across days and leads to groin strains.

**Sleep and nutrition (non-negotiable recovery factors):**
- Minimum eight hours of sleep per night during periods of high training load. Sleep quality is the single highest-impact recovery tool available without equipment.
- Post-session nutrition window: within 45 minutes of the final session whistle. Target 20–30 grams of protein and 60–80 grams of carbohydrate.

**Load monitoring principle:**
- Track weekly training volume (total minutes of on-court work plus gym sessions).
- Do not increase total weekly load by more than 10 percent above the previous week.
- Sudden spikes in weekly load volume are the leading predictor of soft-tissue injury — the 10 percent rule is not conservative, it is evidence-based.

## 12. Sports Psychology — Concentration and Focus

Section summary: This section covers concentration training techniques that are embedded in regular drills, including pre-shot routines, attentional focus cues, and deliberate distraction exposure.

Mental skills are trainable through deliberate practice, just like physical skills. Integrate psychological training into daily sessions rather than treating it as a separate workshop or a pre-game activity.

**Pre-shot routine:**
- Each player develops a personal two-to-three-step routine that anchors attention before a free throw or critical shot.
- Example: one dribble → spin the ball → deep breath → shoot.
- The routine must be consistent in length and sequence — it is the anchor that narrows attention to the immediate task and blocks out irrelevant stimuli.
- Install during Phase 2 shooting drills: require every player to perform their routine before every free throw repetition.

**Attentional focus cue:**
- Train players to name one specific external cue to focus on during each action.
- Examples: "front of the rim" for free throws, "ball and man" for off-ball defence, "elbow at the basket" for layup approach.
- External cues (focusing on the target, not the body movement) are consistently more effective than internal cues (focusing on limb positions) at game speed.

**Distraction control:**
- Deliberately introduce noise and visual distractions during skill drills to simulate game pressure.
- Method: play crowd noise (from a phone or speaker) during free throw drills. Have non-participating players make noise and wave towels.
- This is not a gimmick — deliberate distraction exposure during practice reduces the performance impact of crowd noise during games.

## 13. Sports Psychology — Confidence and Self-Talk

Section summary: This section explains how to build player confidence through mastery rehearsal and how to train process-focused self-talk and error-response sequences that replace outcome anxiety with execution focus.

**Mastery rehearsal:**
- End each skill session with a success. After a challenging drill block, have players finish their last shooting repetitions from close range (Level 1 of the shooting progression) to build a memory of successful execution.
- The brain's last-encoded memory of a skill session has outsized influence on next-session readiness. Ending on a make, not a miss, is a coachable habit.

**Process-focused self-talk:**
- Replace outcome-focused self-talk ("I must make this shot") with process-focused self-talk ("I will follow my mechanics").
- Outcome focus creates anxiety about events outside the player's control (whether the ball goes in). Process focus keeps attention on actions within the player's control (the shooting sequence).
- Teach this explicitly: have players write down their outcome thought and then reframe it as a process thought. Practice the reframe until the process version is automatic.

**Error reframing — three-step response:**
- Teach players a three-step error response to use immediately after any mistake during drills or games:
  1. Acknowledge the error with one neutral word ("OK").
  2. Identify the specific correction needed in three words or fewer ("elbow in," "jump stop," "call the screen").
  3. Visualise the correct action (two to three seconds, eyes closed if appropriate) before the next repetition.
- Do not allow players to dwell on errors for more than five seconds. The three-step response is a time-limited protocol.

## 14. Sports Psychology — Arousal Regulation

Section summary: This section explains how to identify individual optimal arousal profiles and how to apply activation or calming techniques in warm-up and pre-game contexts.

Arousal = physiological and psychological activation level. Every player has an individual optimal arousal zone — the activation level at which they perform best. Both over-arousal (anxiety, panic) and under-arousal (flat, unfocused) impair performance. Know each player's profile.

**Identifying arousal profile:**
- Over-aroused players before high-stakes games: visibly tense muscles, shallow breathing, rapid speech, over-thinking.
- Under-aroused players before high-stakes games: quiet, slow movement, lack of energy, reduced attention.

**Activation techniques (for under-aroused players):**
- High-intensity dynamic warm-up with upbeat music.
- Team chant or hand-clap routine (collective activation is especially effective for under-aroused teams).
- Brief sprint series or explosive jump sequence (four to five box jumps) immediately before entering the court.

**Calming techniques (for over-aroused players):**
- Box breathing: four seconds inhale, hold four seconds, four seconds exhale, hold four seconds. Two to three cycles.
- Progressive muscle relaxation: systematically tense and release each major muscle group in the pre-game locker room.
- Quiet visualisation of a specific game plan action (e.g., visualise executing the first BLOB play of the game successfully from start to finish).

**In-game reset after an error:**
- After a poor possession, missed assignment, or turnover during the game, teach the "reset breath" — one slow exhale before the next play begins.
- This single-breath reset activates the parasympathetic nervous system and clears the short-term emotional reactivity that causes the second consecutive defensive lapse or the second consecutive missed shot.
- Drill the reset breath in practice: after every error in a competitive drill, require the player who erred to take the reset breath visibly before the next rep. Build it as a habit before it is needed in a game.

## 15. Sports Psychology — Team Cohesion and Communication

Section summary: This section covers the four team-cohesion practices — role clarity, conflict resolution, shared goals, and post-game review — that consistently predict sustained team performance above the level of individual talent.

Team cohesion is the strongest predictor of sustained performance above individual talent. A talented but fragmented team underperforms consistently; a cohesive team of moderate talent regularly exceeds expectations.

**Role clarity:**
- Every player must be able to state their primary role on the team in one sentence.
- Examples: "My role is to set hard screens and hold the weak-side corner for kick-out threes." "My role is to guard the opponent's best perimeter player and not turn the ball over."
- Ambiguity about individual roles is the most common source of team-performance dysfunction. Conduct a role-clarity check at the start of each pre-season.

**Constructive conflict resolution:**
- Establish a team norm that disagreements are addressed directly and privately within 24 hours of an incident.
- Public confrontation during practice or games — even if the underlying observation is correct — damages team cohesion more than the original error.
- Coach's role: model direct, non-emotional feedback on individual and team performance. Players learn conflict behaviour from watching the coaching staff.

**Shared process goals:**
- Set one team process goal for each training week (not a win-loss goal — a behaviour goal).
- Examples: "Zero communication errors on defensive rotations this week." "Every player delivers three verbal encouragements per practice this week."
- Process goals are within the team's control. Outcome goals (wins, points) are not. Process goals build the specific habits that eventually produce outcomes.

**Post-game team review:**
- Conduct a 10-minute team review within 24 hours of each game. Not during the game, not in the locker room immediately after — the next day.
- Format: two things the team did well (specific, named, acknowledged), one thing the team will improve before the next game (specific and immediately actionable, with a clear next step).
- Do not exceed 10 minutes. Short, specific reviews are retained; long reviews become noise.
