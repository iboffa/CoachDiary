# Basketball Defensive Systems — Complete Reference

---

## 1. Defensive Philosophy and Culture

Section summary: This section establishes the defensive identity framework that underpins all schemes; non-negotiable principles apply to every possession regardless of which specific scheme is in use.

Defensive identity is built through daily habits, not game-day emphasis. Before installing any defensive scheme, define the team's defensive identity statement — a short, memorable phrase the entire roster can recite — and the three to five non-negotiables that govern every possession. Schemes and alignments will change across opponents and seasons; the non-negotiables do not.

**The five defensive non-negotiables:**

- **Communication:** Every defender calls out screens, switches, ball-movement, and coverage decisions before the action reaches them. Silence on defence is a defensive error — treat it as such in practice.
- **Positioning before athleticism:** A player in the correct defensive position requires less athleticism than a player scrambling to recover from a wrong position. Positioning is a coachable habit; athleticism is not.
- **No straight-line drives:** Defenders force ball-handlers to change direction on every drive attempt. The goal is to add steps to the drive, not to block shots. A blocked shot is a bonus; a forced step-back is the standard.
- **Block-out on every shot:** Every player finds and bodies their nearest opponent before looking for the ball on any shot attempt. Block-outs win games at all competitive levels.
- **Transition defence first:** After any shot attempt, the nearest players retreat immediately. Transition defence takes priority over offensive rebounding for all but the two designated rebounders.

**Defensive identity statement examples:**
- "Hard to score on, harder to stop" (aggressive, trapping philosophy)
- "We make them earn everything" (physical, disciplined man-to-man philosophy)
- "Communicate, rotate, recover" (rotation-based, team-defence philosophy)

## 2. Man-to-Man Defence — On-Ball Principles

Section summary: On-ball defence determines the angle of attack and the forcing direction for the entire defensive structure; every other defender's position is calculated relative to the on-ball defender's forcing direction.

Man-to-man defence assigns each defender a specific offensive player. It is the most versatile system and the foundation from which all other schemes derive. The on-ball defender is responsible for forcing the ball-handler into the pre-agreed forcing direction. If you are coaching on-ball stance, use the following cues.

**Stance mechanics:**
- Feet shoulder-width apart. Knees bent. Weight on the balls of the feet, not the heels.
- Hips are lower than the ball-handler's hips — a lower centre of gravity wins first-step contests.
- Lead hand mirrors the ball — active hand nearest the ball.
- Trail hand is kept low to discourage straight-line drives to the strong side.

**Footwork — lateral defensive slide:**
- Push off the back foot. Slide the lead foot in the direction of movement. Reset with feet shoulder-width.
- Never cross the feet in the path of a live dribbler — a crossed-feet stance can be attacked with a single jab-step.

**Forcing direction:**
- The team must agree on a single forcing direction (baseline or middle) before each game.
- Inconsistency in forcing direction breaks help-side rotations because helpers are positioned on the wrong side.
- Baseline force: on-ball defender's lead foot is on the inside hip of the ball-handler, pushing them toward the sideline or baseline.
- Middle force: lead foot is on the outside hip, channelling the ball-handler toward the paint where help defenders are positioned.

**Ball denial on the wing — one pass away:**
- When the ball-handler is one pass away from the defender's assigned player, the defender uses a denial stance: one foot in the passing lane, vision split between ball and assigned player. This prevents clean wing catches and forces lob passes over the top.

Half-court canvas reference: wing denial position is at approximately {x:230, y:155} or {x:230, y:355}. On-ball position at the top of the key is approximately {x:280, y:255}.

## 3. Man-to-Man Defence — Off-Ball (Help-Side) Principles

Section summary: Off-ball defenders form the help structure that makes on-ball pressure viable; without correct help-side positioning, even excellent on-ball defence results in easy scores.

**Ball-you-man triangle:**
- Every off-ball defender positions themselves so that a straight line can be drawn from their body to both the ball and their assigned player simultaneously. This alignment ensures the defender can see both and react to either.

**Proximity rule — one pass away vs. two passes away:**
- One pass away (the ball can reach the player with a single pass): defender uses denial stance — positioned in the passing lane, one foot forward, arm in the lane.
- Two passes away (the ball requires two passes to reach the player): defender drops toward the lane (approx. {x:150–175, y:255}) in help position. The rule is "one pass away = denial; two passes away = help-side."

**Help-side positioning (weak-side):**
- Off-ball defenders on the weak side drop toward the lane to provide help against drives that split or beat the on-ball defender.
- The help-side defender is positioned so they can stop a drive in the paint without having to sprint more than two lateral steps.
- Approximate help-side position for a wing defender on a WS skip situation: {x:175, y:255} (lane area).

**Stunting:**
- A help-side defender briefly shows their body toward the ball-handler on penetration (a one-to-two-second gesture), then recovers immediately to their assigned player.
- Stunting must not result in the defender leaving their assigned player open for a clean catch. If the defender cannot recover in one step, do not stunt.

**Closeout mechanics:**
- When the ball is skipped to the defender's assigned player, the defender sprints directly toward the ball receiver.
- In the final two metres, the defender shortens their steps (short, choppy steps) to lower their centre of gravity and avoid over-running the ball-handler.
- Arrive at the ball-handler with high hands (to contest a shot) and a low centre of gravity (to be ready to slide on a drive).
- Approximate closeout target position: one arm's length from the ball-handler, between the ball-handler and the basket.

**Avoiding ball-watching:**
- Defenders must resist watching the ball and losing track of their assigned player. Discipline is enforced by requiring every defender to call out every off-ball cut and every screen set near them.

## 4. Man-to-Man Defence — Defending Screens

Section summary: Defending off-ball screens is the most technically complex aspect of man-to-man defence; the four coverage methods below apply to both ball screens (P&R) and off-ball screens (pin-downs, staggers, flare screens).

**Note on P&R coverage:** The four P&R coverage methods (drop, hedge/show, switch, ICE/Blue) and the full three-man rotation system are documented in detail in the separate reference file `pick_and_roll_defensive_coverages.md`. This section covers the team-wide screen-coverage decision framework and off-ball screening defence principles.

**Team screen-coverage pre-game decisions:**
- Against an elite P&R ball-handler (PG / 1) who is not a strong shooter: use drop coverage. Allow the mid-range pull-up while protecting the rim and the roll.
- Against an elite P&R ball-handler who is also an elite shooting big: use switch or modified hedge with immediate recovery. Do not concede catch-and-shoot three-pointers.
- Against off-ball screen actions (stagger screens, pin-downs, flare screens): decide pre-game whether to fight over or go under. Fight over for designated shooters; go under for non-shooters.

**Defending pin-down screens:**
- The screened defender (usually guarding a shooter curling off a big's downscreen) must choose pre-possession: fight over the top or go under.
- Fight over: the defender moves between the screener and the cutter, staying on the cutter's hip throughout the curl. Used against shooters who catch and shoot immediately.
- Go under: the defender allows the cutter to use the screen and recovers on the catch. Used against non-shooters who prefer to drive.
- If the defence switches the pin-down: the big (now guarding the shooter at the perimeter) must close out to the three-point line (approx. {x:175, y:113} or {x:175, y:397}) immediately. A slow closeout gives the shooter time to set their feet.

**Defending stagger screens (two sequential screens for one cutter):**
- The cutter's defender must fight through both screens without losing the cutter's body.
- If the defender is late on the first screen, they must communicate "switch" with the first screener's defender.
- When a switch occurs on a stagger, the smallest defender may end up on a big — call "mismatch" immediately so the team can address it.

## 5. Zone Defence — 2-3 Zone

Section summary: The 2-3 zone is the most common zone in basketball; it protects the paint and lane areas but is vulnerable at the high-post gap and in the corners, requiring active stunting and communication to eliminate these gaps.

**Overview:** Zone defence assigns defenders to areas rather than players. The 2-3 zone places two defenders at the top and three defenders across the baseline. It is most effective against teams with weak perimeter shooting and poor ball movement.

**Personnel assignments:**

- **Top defenders (guards — X1 / PG, X2 / SG):** Responsible for the top of the key and wing-entry passes. X1 pressures the primary ball-handler at the top (approx. {x:280, y:255}). X2 covers the weak-side wing and prevents easy reversals (approx. {x:230, y:355} in help position).
- **Wing defenders (forwards — X3 / SF, X4 / PF):** Cover the wing-to-corner area on their respective sides. X3 covers approximately {x:175, y:113} to {x:54, y:90} on the SS. X4 covers approximately {x:175, y:397} to {x:54, y:420} on the WS. On ball reversal, X3 and X4 rotate quickly across the lane.
- **Middle defender (centre — X5 / C):** Responsible for the paint and high-post area. X5 positions at the front of the restricted area (approx. {x:120, y:255}) and must be active in stunting against corner passes to prevent skip-pass threes.

**Zone slides — ball movement scenarios:**

- **Ball at the top (approx. {x:280, y:255}):** X1 pressures the ball-handler. X2 shades toward WS wing. X3 and X4 deny wing entry. X5 holds the high post.
- **Ball entered to the SS wing (approx. {x:230, y:155}):** X3 closes out to the ball. X1 drops to cover the SS elbow gap (approx. {x:175, y:155}). X2 covers the top of the key. X4 shifts to the lane/block area (approx. {x:87, y:255}). X5 covers the SS block and the paint.
- **Ball entered to the SS corner (approx. {x:54, y:90}):** X3 or X4 closes out hard to the corner. X5 covers the near block (approx. {x:87, y:155}) and the WS block. X1 drops to cover the wing. X2 covers the top of the key.
- **Ball at the high post / free-throw line gap (approx. {x:175, y:255}):** This is the primary vulnerability of the 2-3 zone. When the ball reaches the gap, X5 and one guard (X1 or X2) pinch the ball-handler immediately. The remaining three defenders shrink toward the basket.

**Corner coverage rule:**
- When the ball reaches the corner (approx. {x:54, y:90} or {x:54, y:420}), the nearest wing defender (X3 or X4) closes out hard.
- X5 covers the opposite block (approx. {x:87, y:355}).
- X1 or X2 drops to the near block (approx. {x:87, y:155}) to prevent the low-post entry.

**Drilling the 2-3 zone:**
- Run "ball-movement recognition" drills where the coach calls out pass destinations and the five defenders must slide to correct positions simultaneously. Target fewer than one second of delay per slide.

## 6. Zone Defence — 1-3-1 Zone

Section summary: The 1-3-1 zone is a trapping defence designed to force turnovers from unprepared offences by setting traps on the wings and using an active middle defender to intercept skip passes.

**Overview:** The 1-3-1 zone pressures the ball from the moment it crosses half-court and creates trapping situations on the wings. It is most effective as a change-of-pace scheme against teams that have prepared only for a 2-3 zone or man-to-man.

**Personnel assignments:**

- **Top (X1 — usually point guard / PG / 1):** Pressures the ball-handler from the moment they cross half-court (top of the key area, approx. {x:280, y:255}). X1's primary job is to funnel the ball toward one wing — not to steal it at the top.
- **Wing defenders (X2 — SG / 2, X3 — SF / 3):** Set up at the wing and elbow-extended area (approx. {x:200, y:155} and {x:200, y:355}). When the ball enters the wing, the nearest wing defender closes hard and traps with X1.
- **Middle (X4 — usually athletic forward / PF / 4):** Floats in the middle of the lane (approx. {x:175, y:255}), intercepts skip passes across the zone, and provides help against baseline drives. X4 is the most athletically demanding position in this scheme.
- **Baseline (X5 — usually centre / C / 5):** Covers the baseline and both corners simultaneously (shuttling between approx. {x:54, y:90} and {x:54, y:420}). X5 is responsible for eliminating the easy corner catch against the wing trap.

**Trap timing and execution:**
- The trap is set the moment the ball-handler catches on the wing (approx. {x:230, y:155} or {x:230, y:355}) and has not yet put the ball on the floor, or has picked up the dribble.
- Do not trap a live dribbler in the open floor — wait until the dribbler catches or kills their dribble.
- X1 closes from above the ball; the wing defender (X2 or X3) closes from beside the ball. Hands high, feet outside the ball-handler's feet to form a wall.

**Rotation responsibilities after the trap:**
- X4 (middle) moves toward the most dangerous skip-pass receiver — typically the opposite wing or the high post.
- X5 (baseline) shifts to eliminate the corner or baseline catch on the trapped side.
- The non-trapping wing defender (X2 or X3) drops toward the lane to help against any drive through the trap.

**1-3-1 vulnerabilities:**
- The high post (approx. {x:175, y:255}) is open if X4 is caught out of position.
- The opposite corner (approx. {x:54, y:420}) is open if the ball is reversed quickly.
- Effective against teams with slow ball movement and poor skip-pass execution; less effective against experienced offences that immediately attack the high post.

## 7. Zone Defence — Match-Up Zone

Section summary: The match-up zone combines zone alignments with man-to-man assignments and is the hardest zone to attack because it disguises its coverage until the offence has already committed.

**Overview:** In a match-up zone, defenders start in a zone alignment but convert to man-to-man assignments when the ball or an offensive player enters their zone area. It requires players with high basketball IQ and strong communication skills. It is most effective against teams with weak ball movement or predictable offensive patterns.

**Match rule:**
- Any offensive player who enters a defender's zone area is matched man-to-man until they leave that area or until a rotation call is made by the nearest defender.

**No-match rule:**
- If an offensive player stands still inside a zone area for more than three seconds, the nearest help defender calls "check" and takes the assignment, preventing the defence from being stretched by a stationary player.

**Rotation on penetration:**
- On dribble penetration, the closest help defender takes the driver (rotating toward the ball, approx. {x:120, y:255} paint area).
- Zone responsibilities shift one position in the direction of the ball — every other defender slides one spot in the ball direction.

**Teaching the match-up zone:**
- Begin with the 2-3 zone base as the starting alignment.
- Layer in match rules one at a time: first match the corner, then match the wing, then match the high post.
- Use "shadow" drills where defenders start in zone and must call out the moment they make a man-to-man match.

## 8. Press Defence — Full-Court Man Press

Section summary: The full-court man press applies maximum ball pressure from the inbound, denying clean entry and setting traps within the first five metres of the court; it requires superior conditioning and athleticism to sustain.

**Overview:** Full-court man press applies man-to-man pressure from the baseline inbound pass. It is most effective when the defensive team has superior conditioning and athleticism and the opponent has weak ball-handling depth.

**Inbound denial assignments:**
- Each defender denies their assigned player from receiving the inbound pass. The defender guarding the inbounder positions with both hands up in the passing lane, pressuring the passer.
- The goal is to force a lob pass or a backward pass to a less comfortable ball-handler.

**Post-inbound double-team trigger:**
- When the ball is successfully inbounded, two defenders converge on the ball-handler within the first five metres of the court (near the baseline, approx. {x:54–120, y:255} corridor).
- The trap is set immediately — the ball-handler should not be allowed to pick up speed before the trap closes.

**Trap zones (priority locations):**
- Priority 1: Baseline corners (near {x:54, y:90} or {x:54, y:420}) where the sideline and baseline create natural trapping walls on two sides.
- Priority 2: The mid-court area near the half-court line where the half-court line adds a third boundary.

**Rotation on the trap:**
- When two defenders trap, the remaining three defenders rotate to steal-position on the anticipated passing lanes, not to cover their assigned men.
- The most dangerous outlet is the nearest open receiver — one defender must anticipate and position in that passing lane.

**Foul discipline:**
- Defenders must never reach for the ball from behind on the press. Press defence generates fouls when defenders overreach.
- All physical pressure is directed at the ball-handler's chest and hips — no slapping, no reaching across the body.

## 9. Press Defence — Half-Court Trap (3/4 Press)

Section summary: A three-quarter press applies ball pressure just before half-court, conserving defensive energy while still disrupting the opponent's offensive rhythm and forcing rushed decisions before the set offence is organised.

**Overview:** The 3/4 press activates at the three-quarter-court mark rather than at the inbound. It conserves defensive energy while still disrupting the opponent's ball-movement rhythm before they can set their half-court offence.

**Activation point:**
- Defenders pick up their assignments at the three-quarter-court line (approx. three-quarters of the full court, equivalent to approximately the opponent's free-throw line extended in full-court terms).
- Before the activation point, defenders retreat to their half-court positions.

**Trap trigger:**
- The double-team is set when the ball-handler crosses the half-court line and enters the corners of the court near the sideline (approx. half-court sideline corners).
- Do not trap a ball-handler in the middle of the court — sideline proximity is required to limit the ball-handler's escape routes.

**Recovery after a broken trap:**
- If the trap is broken (ball-handler escapes or passes out of the trap), all five defenders sprint immediately to their half-court defensive positions.
- Do not continue pressing after a trap is beaten — continuing the press while outnumbered is the primary cause of easy transition baskets from press defence.

## 10. Transition Defence (OTO)

Section summary: OTO (Offence-to-Defence transition) is the moment of highest defensive vulnerability; structured sprint-back rules and a three-layer framework convert every shot attempt into an organised defensive retreat.

**Overview:** OTO = Offence-to-Defence transition. This occurs the moment any shot is taken or any turnover happens — the offensive team must immediately reverse into defensive roles. A missed shot with no transition defence in place is the highest-frequency source of easy baskets at all competitive levels.

**Layer 1 — Sprint back (all five players):**
- Every player sprints back to the paint area the moment a shot is taken or a turnover occurs.
- Speed takes priority over assignment accuracy at this layer — getting back before the ball does is the only goal.
- Two designated offensive rebounders may stay at the offensive glass; all other three players retreat immediately.

**Layer 2 — Pick up the most dangerous player:**
- Once back in the half-court area (approx. behind the three-point arc at {x:280, y:255}), each defender picks up the closest offensive player.
- Matching assignments to the correct individual is corrected verbally within two seconds by the point guard (PG / 1) calling out position corrections.

**Layer 3 — Take away the lay-up first:**
- The first defender back positions themselves at the basket (approx. {x:80, y:255}) to prevent a straight-line drive to the rim.
- This first defender is not required to hold their position against a two-on-one; their job is to influence the angle of attack and delay the ball-handler until help arrives.
- Do not gamble for a steal in transition — the risk of a foul or a blown assignment outweighs the reward.

**Transition defence vs. offensive rebounding priority:**
- At competitive levels, designate exactly two offensive rebounders per possession. Every other player's first instinct after a shot is to sprint toward their defensive half.
- At youth levels (U14/U16), simplify the rule: "three retreat, two rebound" — any three players not holding the ball at the moment of the shot sprint back.

## 11. In-Game Defensive Adjustments

Section summary: Effective defensive coaching requires halftime and timeout adjustments based on observed opponent tendencies; this section provides a decision framework for the six most common in-game defensive problems.

**If the opponent is scoring from three-point range:**
- Switch to a tighter closeout protocol: defenders must arrive at three-point shooters with feet set and no momentum — no running by on the closeout.
- Consider switching from drop coverage (P&R) to hedge on all pick-and-roll actions to eliminate the pull-up three.
- Communicate "ghost the shooter" to ensure every off-ball defender knows where the opponent's best shooter is at all times.

**If the opponent is scoring in transition:**
- Increase the number of mandatory retreat players from three to four per possession (only one designated rebounder).
- Audit whether offensive rebounders are staying too long after missed shots — sprint-back timing is the primary cause of transition points given up.

**If the opponent exploits zone gaps (high-post or corner):**
- Shift to man-to-man for two to three consecutive possessions to disrupt the opponent's gap-exploitation rhythm.
- After the shift, return to zone — the rhythm disruption is the goal, not the scheme change.
- Alternatively, adjust zone slides: have X5 flash higher to the free-throw line when the ball is at the top, closing the gap before the pass arrives.

**If the opponent has one dominant scorer:**
- Assign the best individual defender on the roster to that player for the entire game.
- Instruct the assigned defender to front the post (position between the ball and the dominant scorer at the block, approx. {x:87, y:155} or {x:87, y:355}) and deny the ball entirely.
- Provide weak-side help coverage to prevent lob passes over the front.

**If the opponent has poor ball movement:**
- Intensify on-ball pressure — the ball-handler is being asked to create individually. Make that as difficult as possible.
- Use the 1-3-1 zone or a full-court press to force live-ball turnovers before the opponent can run any set.

**If the opponent makes a halftime offensive adjustment:**
- Identify the specific change during the break (new play, new spacing, new entry point).
- Assign one specific defensive counter for that change. Do not change the entire defensive scheme — change one coverage rule for the adjusted action.
- Communicate the adjustment in one sentence: "Against their new stagger entry, we switch — both screens, every time."
