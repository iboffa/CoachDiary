# Pick-and-Roll Defensive Coverages — Complete Defensive Reference

---

## 1. Overview — Coverage Is a Strategic Choice

Section summary: This section explains why pick-and-roll defensive coverage is a pre-determined strategic decision based on personnel and opponent tendencies, not a reactive improvisation.

Defending the pick-and-roll (P&R) is the central challenge of modern half-court basketball. The coverage a team chooses is **not a reaction** made in the moment — it is a pre-game or pre-possession decision made by the coaching staff based on:

- **Own personnel:** Can your big (C/5 or PF/4) switch onto a guard? Can your guard (PG/1) guard a rolling big in the paint?
- **Opponent tendencies:** Is the ball-handler (PG/1) an elite driver or an elite pull-up shooter? Is the screener (C/5) a roll threat or a pop threat?
- **Game situation:** Shot clock, score margin, foul trouble, and personnel on the floor all shift which coverage is optimal.

Every coverage below has structural strengths and structural weaknesses. A well-coached team drills 2–3 primary coverages so players can execute them automatically and knows exactly which offensive counters to expect.

### General P&R defensive principles (apply to all coverages)

- The on-ball defender (X1) must communicate the screen: "Screen right!" or "Screen left!" before contact.
- The screener's defender (X5) must respond with the coverage call: "Drop!", "Hedge!", "Switch!", "Ice!", or "Blitz!"
- All five defenders must know the coverage before the ball-handler uses the screen.
- Weak-side defenders must shrink to help positions whenever the ball drives off the screen.

## 2. Drop Coverage

Section summary: This section defines drop coverage, explains its execution mechanics, identifies when to use it, and describes its strengths, weaknesses, and the offensive counter.

### Definition

In **drop coverage**, the screener's defender (X5) retreats below the level of the screen — typically to the level of the free-throw lane (approx. x:175, y:255) or deeper — to protect the paint and prevent the roll from being productive. X1 fights over the top of the screen independently.

### Execution mechanics

- X5 calls "Drop!" as the screen is being set.
- X5 steps backward (toward the basket) to approximately x:175, y:255, staying between the roller and the hoop.
- X5 does not step out to engage the ball-handler under any circumstances.
- X1 goes over the top of the screen, staying on the ball-handler's hip, and re-engages quickly.
- X5 is responsible for any roll catch inside the paint; X1 re-contains the ball-handler at the perimeter.

### When to use

- Opponent's screener (C/5) is **not** a three-point shooting threat (rim-runner only).
- Own big (X5) is a poor lateral mover — cannot hedge or recover safely.
- Team prioritises eliminating paint points above all else.
- Early shot clock or against a weaker ball-handling team.

### Strengths

- No foul risk from X5 — no aggressive movement near the ball-handler.
- Protects the paint against the roll and the lob.
- Conserves X5's energy — less lateral movement required.
- Easy to teach and communicate — minimal coordination needed.

### Weaknesses

- Gives the ball-handler a wide-open pull-up mid-range or three-point shot (the "Gap" shot) from approximately x:200, y:255.
- If the screener pops to the three-point line, X5 cannot close out quickly enough.
- Elite pull-up guards will abuse drop coverage repeatedly.

### Offensive counter

- Ball-handler (PG/1) uses the screen and immediately pulls up for the gap three or the pull-up mid-range (approx. x:200–230, y:255).
- Screener pops to the three-point line, stretching X5 into an impossible closeout situation.

## 3. Hedge / Show Coverage

Section summary: This section defines hedge/show coverage, explains its execution mechanics, identifies when to use it, and describes its strengths, weaknesses, and the offensive counter.

### Definition

In **hedge (show) coverage**, the screener's defender (X5) steps out aggressively beyond the screen — into the ball-handler's path — to slow or stop penetration. X5 does not switch; instead, X5 "shows" their body to force the ball-handler to pick up their dribble or change direction, then recovers back to their own assignment (C/5) as X1 re-engages.

### Execution mechanics

- X5 calls "Hedge!" or "Show!" before the screen is set.
- As the ball-handler (PG/1) comes off the screen, X5 steps out laterally to approximately x:230, y:255 — one step beyond the screen position.
- X5's hips face the sideline (parallel to the three-point arc), not the basket, to avoid over-committing and giving up the lob.
- X5 holds the hedge for 1–2 counts — long enough to make PG/1 hesitate or pick up the dribble.
- X1 sprints around the screen and re-engages the ball-handler from behind.
- X5 retreats back to the roll path the moment X1 re-engages.

### When to use

- Opponent's ball-handler (PG/1) is an **elite pull-up shooter** who exploits drop coverage repeatedly.
- Own big (X5) is athletic and a strong lateral mover.
- Team has a large height/length advantage that allows X5 to bother the pull-up without fouling.

### Strengths

- Prevents the pull-up mid-range and three-point shot off the screen.
- Forces the ball-handler to make a decision quickly under physical pressure.
- Maintains X5's assignment responsibility — no permanent mismatch created.

### Weaknesses

- When X5 steps out, the roll-to-rim lane is temporarily vacated — the lob pass over X5's outstretched arm is the primary weakness.
- A skip pass to the weak-side wing during X5's hedge leaves a shooter open (approx. x:330, y:113 or x:330, y:397).
- X1's recovery around the screen must be extremely fast — any delay leaves PG/1 unguarded.
- High energy cost for X5 — constant lateral hedging and recovery is exhausting over 48 minutes.

### Offensive counter

- Ball-handler (PG/1) freezes, forces X5 to fully commit, then lobs over X5's arm to the rolling C/5 (target at approx. x:54–80, y:255).
- Skip pass to the weak-side corner shooter during the hedge window (approx. x:87, y:420).

## 4. Switch Coverage

Section summary: This section defines switch coverage, explains its execution mechanics, identifies when to use it, and describes its strengths, weaknesses, and the offensive counter.

### Definition

In **switch coverage**, X1 and X5 exchange assignments permanently when the screen is set. X5 takes the ball-handler (PG/1) and X1 takes the screener (C/5) for the remainder of the possession.

### Execution mechanics

- Communication is **mandatory before the screen is set** — either X1 calls "Switch!" or X5 calls "Switch!" and both confirm.
- X5 steps up to contain the ball-handler at the screen point (approx. x:230, y:255) immediately after the screen contact.
- X1 opens to the interior and locates C/5, taking the roll or pop responsibility.
- Neither defender fights over or under the screen — the switch eliminates the screen entirely.
- Switching teams must drill communication extensively — a missed switch call leaves the ball-handler wide open.

### When to use

- Roster is composed of **positionally versatile, similarly-sized defenders** (modern "positionless" rosters).
- Opponent's screener (C/5) is **not** a post mismatch threat — switching a guard onto the big is viable.
- Late shot clock situations when a hedge or drop requires more coordination than time allows.
- Against complex off-ball actions (stagger screens, DHO chains) where switching prevents confusion.

### Strengths

- Eliminates all screen advantages — the ball-handler is immediately guarded after the screen.
- No gaps in coverage at the moment of the screen.
- Low communication burden after the initial call — simple and clean.
- Effective against teams that chain multiple P&R actions.

### Weaknesses

- **Mismatches are created** — if a slow big (X5) must guard an elite guard (PG/1) on the perimeter, the offence will hunt that mismatch.
- A small guard (X1) guarding the rolling centre (C/5) in the post is an even worse mismatch for the defence.
- Teams will intentionally use the P&R as a mismatch-hunting tool against switching defences.
- Over-reliance on switching reduces individual defensive development.

### Offensive counter

- Ball-handler (PG/1) immediately attacks the slower big (X5) on the dribble (approx. drive to x:120, y:255) — the mismatch is the play.
- If X1 is switched onto C/5, PG/1 feeds the post (approx. x:87, y:210 low block) and C/5 posts up for the mismatch inside.
- Run "mismatch isolation" — clear out one side and let the mismatched player operate 1-on-1.

## 5. ICE / Blue Coverage

Section summary: This section defines ICE (Blue) coverage, explains its execution mechanics, identifies when to use it, and describes its strengths, weaknesses, and the offensive counter.

### Definition

In **ICE coverage** (also called "Blue" by some programmes), the on-ball defender (X1) forces the ball-handler **away from the screen** before it is set, directing PG/1 toward the sideline or baseline. The screener's defender (X5) stays in the paint, protecting the middle of the floor. The screen is effectively neutralised because the ball-handler is never allowed to use it.

### Execution mechanics

- X5 calls "Ice!" or "Blue!" to signal X1 before the ball-handler initiates the action.
- X1 positions themselves on the **inside hip** of PG/1 — the hip closest to the paint — forcing the dribble toward the sideline.
- X1 does **not** go over the screen; instead, they deny the screen side entirely.
- As PG/1 is directed baseline, X5 sinks toward the baseline corner (approx. x:120, y:90 or x:120, y:420) to cut off the drive.
- X1 recovers on the baseline side and re-engages PG/1 along the sideline.
- The screener (C/5) sets the screen but is left momentarily unguarded — X5 must prevent the C/5 roll into the open paint.

### When to use

- **Side P&R or wing P&R** (approx. x:200, y:150 or x:200, y:360) — sideline proximity makes the Ice trap effective.
- Opponent's ball-handler prefers to use the screen going toward the middle.
- Own big (X5) is effective at protecting baseline paint.
- Opponent's screener (C/5) is **not** a short-corner shooter — otherwise X5 vacating the elbow creates a shooting opportunity.

### Strengths

- Prevents the roll directly to the rim because C/5 rolls into X5's coverage zone.
- Forces the ball-handler into a less comfortable direction (baseline).
- Eliminates the ball-handler's preferred read.
- Works naturally with the sideline as an additional defender.

### Weaknesses

- The baseline drive — X5 must cover a lot of ground to the corner if PG/1 beats X1 baseline.
- If C/5 pops to the short corner or elbow after X5 vacates to baseline, C/5 is open (approx. x:175, y:175 elbow area).
- Against a ball-handler who is comfortable going baseline (left-hand dominant), the Ice may not be a deterrent.
- Requires X1 to read the screen direction early — late recognition makes the technique ineffective.

### Offensive counter

- Ball-handler (PG/1) rejects the Ice — reverses direction and attacks middle before X1 can adjust (reject the screen, go opposite).
- Screener (C/5) slips to the elbow or short corner vacated by X5 (approx. x:175, y:175) for a mid-range shot.
- Screener pops to the three-point line immediately — X5 cannot close out from the baseline.

## 6. Blitz / Trap Coverage

Section summary: This section defines blitz/trap coverage, explains its execution mechanics, identifies when to use it, and describes its strengths, weaknesses, and the offensive counter.

### Definition

In **blitz (trap) coverage**, both X1 and X5 aggressively trap the ball-handler (PG/1) at the moment the screen is set — two defenders on one ball-handler. The trap is designed to force a turnover or a rushed, low-percentage decision.

### Execution mechanics

- X5 calls "Blitz!" or "Trap!" and both X1 and X5 confirm.
- As PG/1 uses the screen, X5 steps out hard (beyond the screen) and X1 closes from behind — together they form a trap at approximately x:220, y:255 (screen location).
- X5 and X1 both put hands high, contesting any pass without fouling.
- The trap must close quickly — PG/1 must be forced to make a decision in under 2 seconds.
- **Three remaining defenders (X2, X3, X4) must immediately rotate** to cover the open players:
  - One defender (X3) covers the rolling C/5 (most dangerous open player near the rim).
  - One defender (X2 or X4) rotates to the nearest perimeter shooter.
  - One defender (X4 or X2) plays in the passing lane on the weak side, ready to intercept.

### When to use

- Opponent has an **elite ball-handler** (PG/1) who exploits individual defence consistently.
- Late-game situations where a forced turnover is more valuable than a contested shot.
- Team has long, active weak-side defenders who can rotate and recover.
- Short-clock situations where any disruption is valuable.

### Strengths

- Forces the ball-handler to make a rapid decision under physical pressure — increases turnover rate.
- Disrupts timing: the screener (C/5) does not get the ball at the expected time, breaking P&R rhythm.
- Psychologically pressures the ball-handler.

### Weaknesses

- Creates a **4v3 situation** if the trap is beaten with a quick pass — the offence has a player advantage.
- If PG/1 is a skilled passer, the blitz will lead directly to an open three-pointer or rim finish.
- Extremely high energy cost — cannot be used on every possession.
- Requires all five defenders to rotate in coordination; one mistake leads to an easy basket.

### Offensive counter

- PG/1 must recognise the blitz early (before picking up the dribble) and pass out of the trap immediately.
- Primary target: the rolling C/5, who has an unobstructed path to the rim (approx. x:80, y:255) since X5 has left the roll to trap.
- Secondary target: the corner shooter on either side (approx. x:87, y:90 or x:87, y:420) — the 4v3 advantage makes a corner three-pointer available.
- PG/1 should use a third dribble escape (sidestep) if the trap closes before they can pass.

## 7. Three-Man Rotation

Section summary: This section defines the three-man rotation concept, explains when it is required, details the execution sequence, and assigns communication responsibilities to each defender.

### Definition

The **three-man rotation** is a help-side defensive system that activates when the ball-handler beats their defender or when X5 steps out on a hedge/blitz, temporarily vacating the roll lane. Three defenders must rotate in sequence to cover the roller and prevent an easy basket, while ensuring no perimeter shooter is left completely open.

### When it is needed

- Whenever X5 steps out (hedge, show, blitz) and the ball-handler beats X1 off the dribble simultaneously.
- When a switch leaves a mismatched defender at a disadvantage and help is required.
- During transition defence when off-ball assignments have not been established yet.

### Execution sequence

1. **X5 steps out** on the hedge, vacating the roll-to-rim lane.
2. **Weak-side helper (X3 or X4)** recognises the vacated lane and rotates to cover the rolling C/5 — pinching toward the paint (approx. x:120, y:255 to x:80, y:255).
3. **X5 recovers** from the hedge back toward C/5's original location — but since X3/X4 has covered the roll, X5 now rotates to cover the assignment vacated by X3/X4 (e.g., the weak-side wing at approx. x:330, y:113).
4. **X1 re-engages** the ball-handler after sprinting around the screen.
5. All rotations complete before the ball-handler makes their next decision.

### Communication responsibilities

- **X5:** Must call the coverage ("Hedge!") and verbally signal recovery: "Got ball!" when re-engaging or "Rotate!" to trigger the three-man movement.
- **X3/X4 (weak-side helper):** Must verbalise "Got roller!" when taking the C/5 responsibility.
- **X2 (weak-side perimeter):** Must call "Stay!" or "Shifting!" to confirm they are maintaining perimeter coverage and not collapsing unnecessarily.
- **General rule:** Every rotation must be spoken, not just executed — silent rotations lead to double coverage on one player and no coverage on another.

## 8. Coverage Selection Guide

Section summary: This section provides a decision table to help coaching staff select the appropriate pick-and-roll defensive coverage based on opponent personnel, game situation, and own roster capabilities.

Use this table as a starting point. Coverage may be mixed across possessions or quarters based on game flow.

| Situation | Recommended Coverage | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Opponent has **elite ball-handler / pull-up shooter** | Hedge or Blitz | Prevents the gap shot; forces a quick decision under pressure |
| Opponent has **elite shooting big (stretch 5/4)** | Switch or Drop | Drop keeps X5 near the rim; Switch puts a capable defender on the shooter |
| **Side P&R or wing P&R** | ICE / Blue | Sideline proximity makes the force-baseline technique viable |
| Own roster has **limited lateral athleticism at C/5** | Drop | Avoids X5 being caught out of position on a hedge or switch |
| Own roster is **positionally versatile (switchable)** | Switch | Eliminates the screen entirely when mismatches are tolerable |
| **Late shot clock / short time** | Blitz or Switch | Forces a rushed decision; cleaner communication than hedge |
| Opponent chains **DHO + P&R combos** | Switch | Prevents confusion from layered actions |
| Opponent's screener **never pops (always rolls)** | Drop or Hedge | Drop protects the rim; Hedge contests the drive without switching |
| **Foul trouble on X5** | Drop | Avoids X5 contesting aggressively near the ball-handler |
| **Zone offence attack is the primary threat** | ICE or Drop | Reduces the P&R's effectiveness when combined with zone principles |
